The Age of Brain Rot

A New Kind of Internet Culture

In late 2024, the Oxford University Press named a strange, two-word phrase as its Word of the Year: "Brain rot". By the time we reached 2026, the term had become a permanent part of the global vocabulary, used by everyone from middle school students in Taiwan to university professors in London. But what exactly is brain rot? To some, it is just a funny way to describe silly videos. To others, it is a serious warning about how we use our phones and how technology might be changing our minds.

What is Brain Rot?

"Brain rot" (often written as one word: brainrot) is a slang term used to describe the feeling of being "mentally tired" or "foggy" after spending too much time online. It specifically refers to the consumption of low-quality, repetitive, and absurd content.

For the younger generations, known as Generation Z and Generation Alpha, brain rot is also a specific style of internet humor. This style is often chaotic, fast-paced, and filled with strange words that make no sense to older people. Famous examples include the "Skibidi Toilet" series, the "Only in Ohio" memes, and terms like "Rizz" (meaning charisma) or "Gyatt". While these videos are often watched for entertainment, the term "brain rot" ironically suggests that watching them is so useless that it feels like your brain is actually rotting away.

The History: From a Cabin to a Smartphone

Most people think "brain rot" is a modern invention of the TikTok era, but the term is actually over 170 years old. The first recorded use of the phrase was in 1854 by the American writer Henry David Thoreau in his famous book, Walden. Thoreau lived in a small cabin in the woods to find a simpler life, and he worried that society was becoming too focused on simple, trivial ideas. He asked if anyone would try to "cure the brain-rot" that he saw spreading through the minds of people who didn't think deeply.

The term reappeared on Twitter in 2007 to describe reality TV shows and video games. However, it didn't truly explode in popularity until the COVID-19 lockdowns of 2020. With the world stuck at home, millions of people began "doomscrolling"—endlessly scrolling through negative or mindless news and videos. Between 2023 and 2024, the use of the term increased by 230%, leading to its crowning as the Word of the Year.

Why Did It Become So Famous?

Brain rot content became a global phenomenon because of three main factors: technology, social bonding, and the "Fear of Missing Out" (FOMO).

  • The Algorithm and the Infinite Scroll: Apps like TikTok and Instagram use "infinite scrolling," which means the content never ends. The computer programs (algorithms) are designed to learn exactly what you like and keep showing you more of it, making it very difficult to put the phone down.
  • Dopamine Hits: Every time you see a new, funny video, your brain releases a small chemical called dopamine. This is the brain's "reward" chemical. Short-form videos provide these rewards every 15 to 30 seconds, creating an addictive loop where your brain constantly craves the next video.
  • Social Connection: For many young people, using "brain rot" slang is a way to feel included in a group. If you don't know what "Skibidi" or "Fanum Tax" means, you might feel left out of the conversation. This "FOMO" drives people to stay online for hours to stay updated on the latest weird trends.

The Potential Damages to the Brain

While "brain rot" is often used as a joke, doctors and scientists are concerned about the real cognitive effects of overexposure to this content.

  • Attention Span Decline: Constant exposure to fast-switching videos trains the brain to expect information in tiny, rapid bursts. This is sometimes called "TikTok Brain" or "Popcorn Brain." It makes it much harder to focus on "slow" tasks like reading a book, studying for a test, or sitting through a long lecture.
  • Weakening the Prefrontal Cortex: This is the part of the brain responsible for decision-making, planning, and impulse control. Research suggests that constant digital overstimulation can weaken this area, making it harder for people (especially children) to control their urges or plan for the future.
  • Dopamine Desensitization: When the brain is flooded with dopamine from screens all day, the brain's receptors become less sensitive. This means you need more and more stimulation just to feel "normal." Activities that used to be fun, like walking in nature or talking with friends, might start to feel boring in comparison.
  • Mental Fatigue and Fog: Many students report feeling "drained" or "foggy" after hours of scrolling. This mental fatigue can lead to lower grades, irritability when away from the phone, and even a higher risk of anxiety or depression.

Is the Damage Permanent?

The good news is that the human brain is very adaptable, which is a concept known as neuroplasticity. This means the effects of "brain rot" are likely not permanent. By setting time limits, choosing "long-form" content like books or podcasts, and taking breaks to interact with the real world, the brain can "re-train" itself to focus and find joy in slower, more meaningful activities.

A New Kind of Internet Culture

In late 2024, the famous Oxford University Press chose a very strange, two-word phrase as its official "Word of the Year": "Brain rot". By the year 2026, this term had completely evolved from an obscure internet joke into a permanent part of our global vocabulary. Today, it is used by almost everyone, from middle school students navigating social media in Taiwan to university professors analyzing language changes in London.

But what exactly is brain rot? To some people, it is just a funny, casual way to describe the silly, chaotic, and crazy videos we see on our feeds. To other people, it serves as a serious, urgent warning about how we use our smartphones every single day. It forces us to ask deep questions about how modern technology might be changing our minds, our habits, and how we think about the world around us.

What is Brain Rot?

At its core, "brain rot" is a slang term. People use it to describe that very specific feeling of being "mentally tired," exhausted, or "foggy" after spending far too much time scrolling on the internet. It does not just mean using a computer for homework; it specifically refers to watching low-quality, highly repetitive, and completely absurd digital content for hours at a time.

For younger generations, such as Generation Z and Generation Alpha, brain rot has also become a unique style of internet humor. This style of content is fast-paced, loud, and filled with strange words. In fact, these phrases usually make absolutely no sense to older people like parents or teachers. Some of the most famous examples that dominate the internet include: The "Skibidi Toilet" series: A surreal, chaotic video series about toilets with human heads that became a massive global trend among kids. The "Only in Ohio" memes: A long-running joke used to describe bizarre, impossible, or completely wild situations as if they only happen in one specific place. Slang terms like "Rizz" or "Gyatt": Brand-new vocabulary words where "Rizz" means having charisma or charm, and "Gyatt" is used as an exclamation of surprise.

While teenagers and kids watch these short clips for fun and entertainment, the term "brain rot" ironically suggests that watching them is totally useless. It feels like your brain is actually rotting away while you sit and watch them.

Why Did It Become So Famous?

Brain rot content became a massive global phenomenon because of three main reasons: technology, social bonding, and FOMO.

  • 1. The Algorithm and the Infinite Scroll: Apps like TikTok and Instagram use "infinite scrolling," meaning the videos automatically play and the page never ends. The computer programs behind these apps, called algorithms, watch your behavior to learn exactly what you like. By instantly showing you more of your favorite topics, they create a customized feed that makes it incredibly difficult to put your phone down.
  • 2. Dopamine Hits: Every time you see a funny or surprising video, your brain releases a tiny amount of a "reward" chemical called dopamine, making you feel happy for a brief moment. Short-form videos are powerful because they deliver these chemical rewards every 15 to 30 seconds. This quickly creates an addictive loop where your brain constantly craves the next quick laugh, keeping you trapped on the app.
  • 3. Social Connection and FOMO: For many students, using "brain rot" slang is a way to feel included. Inside school hallways and group chats, these memes form a shared language. If you don't know what words like "Skibidi" or "Fanum Tax" mean, you might feel left out. This powerful feeling is called "FOMO" (Fear of Missing Out), and it forces people to stay online for hours just to keep up with the latest trends.

The Potential Damages to the Brain

Although most people use the word "brain rot" as a harmless joke, doctors, psychologists, and scientists are very worried about the real cognitive effects it has on our health.

  • Attention Span Decline: Watching fast, chaotic videos trains the brain to expect information in tiny, rapid bursts, a problem often called "TikTok Brain" or "Popcorn Brain." Because the brain gets used to constant excitement, students find it much harder to focus on "slow" everyday tasks like reading a textbook, studying for an exam, or sitting through a long school lecture.
  • Weakening the Prefrontal Cortex: The prefrontal cortex is the front part of the brain responsible for decision-making, planning, and impulse control. Research suggests that constant digital overstimulation can weaken this critical area. When this happens, it becomes much harder for children and teenagers to control their actions, resist distractions, or stay organized.
  • Dopamine Desensitization: When bright screens flood the brain with dopamine all day, the brain's natural receptors become less sensitive. Because your brain gets used to this high stimulation, you need more and more screen time just to feel normal. As a result, fun real-world activities like walking in nature, playing sports, or talking with friends face-to-face start to feel incredibly boring.
  • Mental Fatigue and Fog: Many students report feeling completely "drained," exhausted, or "foggy" after scrolling through social media for hours. This deep mental fatigue can cause lower school grades because the brain cannot retain information. It also leads to bad moods and irritability when you are forced to step away from your phone, and it can even create a much higher risk of long-term anxiety or depression.

Is the Damage Permanent?

Thankfully, the good news is that the human brain is incredibly flexible, powerful, and adaptable. This amazing scientific concept is known as neuroplasticity.

Neuroplasticity means that the negative side effects of "brain rot" are not permanent. Your brain has the ability to heal, change, and grow stronger over time. By setting daily time limits on your apps, choosing "long-form" content like reading physical books or listening to educational podcasts, and spending more time interacting with the real world, the brain can slowly "re-train" itself. With patience and better habits, you can teach your mind to focus deeply once again and find true, lasting joy in slower, more meaningful activities.
